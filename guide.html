Chapter 01

Choose a topic you can actually finish

The Real-World Problem: Most delayed theses stall at month three because the student chose a grand, philosophical topic without checking whether participants, medical records, or data files actually exist.

Most thesis problems begin with a topic that is too big. Test every candidate topic against three ruthless criteria before committing:

  • Answerable: Can data or empirical evidence settle it, rather than just opinion?
  • Measurable: Can you explicitly name the variables, scales, or qualitative themes you will collect?
  • Doable: Can you finish it with your time, physical access, and budget?

The Narrowing Funnel (Step by Step)

Example narrowing walkthrough:
× Too broad: Hospital waste management and environmental health.
∼ Narrower: Clinical waste practices in operating theatres of private hospitals.
✓ Actionable: Waste segregation protocol compliance among scrub nurses in tertiary private hospitals of Lahore.

Chapter 02

Turn it into a clear research question

The Real-World Problem: Students often write poetic or philosophical questions ("How can hospital culture be improved?") that cannot be answered with statistical tests or qualitative coding frames.

A rigorous empirical question explicitly identifies the independent variable, dependent variable, and target population. Use these frames and fill in the blanks:

Frame 1 • Comparison: Does [Dependent Variable] differ significantly between [Group A] and [Group B] among [Population]?

Frame 2 • Relationship: Is there a significant relationship between [Variable 1] and [Variable 2] among [Population]?

Frame 3 • Prevalence / Description: What is the prevalence of [Condition/Behavior] among [Population] across [Setting]?

Keep objectives matched: The Alignment Matrix

The single most common reason examiners fail proposals is methodological drift — when Chapter 3 uses tests that do not answer the questions in Chapter 1. Fill one row per objective:

ObjectiveHypothesisVariable(s) & scaleTest or methodTarget Table
1. Compare test scores between groupsH1: Group A will score higher than BScore (Continuous ratio scale)Independent samples t-testTable 4.1
2. Determine link between stress & sleepH2: Significant negative correlationStress (Scale), Sleep (Hours)Pearson r correlationTable 4.2
3. Assess compliance association by shiftH3: Association between shift & complianceShift (Nominal), Compliance (Binary)Chi-square test of independenceTable 4.3

Checks before you finalise

  • Each objective starts with one measurable verb (compare, measure, identify, assess; avoid "understand" or "explore").
  • The hypothesis can be proved wrong by your data.
  • The population and setting are stated in the question.
  • Every objective generates its own dedicated table or chart in your Results chapter.
Common Mistake: Stating five objectives in Chapter 1, but only presenting three tables in Chapter 4. Examiners cross-reference these page numbers immediately.

Chapter 03

Search the literature properly

The Real-World Problem: Relying solely on Google Scholar page one, citing predatory journals, or copying citations from third-party bibliographies without reading the primary source.

Search academic databases with a reproducible plan and keep a log:

Constructing Boolean Search Strings

Combine ideas with AND, synonyms with OR, and exact phrases in quotes:

("biomedical waste" OR "healthcare waste" OR "clinical waste") AND (segregation OR compliance OR practices) AND ("operating theatre" OR "operating room")

Reference-manager setup (Zotero / Mendeley / EndNote)

  1. Pick one tool and stay with it throughout the thesis.
  2. Import via direct DOI / PMID lookup to avoid manual typing errors.
  3. Add a tag for each chapter that uses the source (e.g. Ch2_Prevalence).
  4. Attach the real PDF to every entry. Never cite an abstract alone.
  5. Fix author names and years in the manager, never by hand in the Word draft.

Chapter 04

Pick a design and a sample size

The Real-World Problem: Students pulling arbitrary numbers like "100 participants" out of thin air without mathematical justification or statistical power analysis.

Your question decides your method, not personal preference:

Design TypeWhen to UseData CollectedTypical Tools
Quantitative DescriptiveMeasuring prevalence, baseline characteristics, attitudes.Numerical survey scores, demographic data.Validated Likert scales, questionnaires.
Quantitative Quasi-ExperimentalTesting pre-post interventions without random allocation.Pre-test and post-test scores across cohorts.Paired measurements, standardized tests.
Qualitative PhenomenologicalUnderstanding lived experiences and perspectives.Audio transcripts, reflective field notes.Semi-structured interview guides.
Mixed Methods ExplanatoryUsing qualitative interviews to explain surprising statistical results.Initial quantitative survey followed by focused interviews.Questionnaire + purposive sub-sample.

1. Survey Proportion Sample Size (Cochran's Formula)

For estimating prevalence or proportions in large or unknown populations:

n = (Z² * p * (1 - p)) / d²

• Z = 1.96 (for a standard 95% Confidence Interval)
• p = 0.50 (assumed proportion for maximum variability)
• d = 0.05 (margin of error at 5%)
Calculation: (1.96² * 0.5 * 0.5) / 0.05² = 384.16 → Minimum 385 participants.

2. Statistical Power Analysis via G*Power

For hypothesis testing (e.g. comparing two independent means with a t-test):

  • Test family: t tests → Means: Difference between two independent means.
  • Type of power analysis: A priori: Compute required sample size.
  • Input Parameters: Effect size d = 0.50 (medium effect), α err prob = 0.05, Power (1-β) = 0.80, Allocation ratio N2/N1 = 1.
  • Output Result: Sample size group 1 = 64, Sample size group 2 = 64 → Total sample size = 128.

Institutional Ethics Clearance Checklist

  1. Participant Information Sheet (PIS) written in plain, accessible language.
  2. Written Informed Consent Form with voluntary withdrawal clause.
  3. Data de-identification plan (storing survey keys separately from responses).
  4. Secure encrypted storage declaration and institutional approval letter.

Chapter 05

Analyse your data without panic (SPSS & SmartPLS)

The Real-World Problem: Jumping directly to running t-tests and regressions in SPSS on raw survey imports without checking missing values, reverse coding, or normality assumptions.

Before any test, clean the data. Most wrong results come from messy data, not hard statistics:

  • Variable View Definition: Verify every item has explicit Values defined (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), appropriate Measure setting (Nominal, Ordinal, Scale), and clean Variable Names.
  • Missing Values: Run Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Frequencies. Screen missing entries; exclude pairwise or impute mean only if missing rate is below 5%. Never hide missing data.
  • Outliers: Generate boxplots: Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Explore. Inspect extreme values. Screen for data-entry typos (e.g. typing "55" instead of "5").
  • Assumptions: Check normality (Shapiro-Wilk p > .05, skewness ±1.5) and spread (Levene's test p > .05) before interpreting parametric tests.

Parametric vs. Non-Parametric Match Guide

Research ObjectiveNormal / Scale (Parametric)Skewed / Ordinal (Non-Parametric)
Compare 2 independent groupsIndependent samples t-testMann-Whitney U test
Compare same group twice (pre/post)Paired samples t-testWilcoxon signed-rank test
Compare 3 or more groupsOne-way ANOVAKruskal-Wallis H test
Relationship between 2 continuous itemsPearson correlation (r)Spearman rank correlation (rho)
Association between 2 categorical itemsChi-square test of independenceFisher's exact test (if cell < 5)

Menu-by-Menu SPSS Execution Paths

  • Independent t-test: Analyze > Compare Means > Independent-Samples T Test. Outcome to Test Variable(s), grouping factor to Grouping Variable → Define Groups (1, 2).
  • One-Way ANOVA: Analyze > Compare Means > One-Way ANOVA. Select Post Hoc > Tukey, and Options > Homogeneity of variance test.
  • Pearson Correlation: Analyze > Correlate > Bivariate. Select Pearson, Two-tailed, Flag significant correlations.
  • Chi-Square: Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Crosstabs. Under Statistics check Chi-square and Phi and Cramer's V; under Cells check Observed and Expected.

Ready-to-Adapt Result Reporting Templates

Independent t-test: An independent-samples t-test was conducted to compare scores between Group A (M = [M1], SD = [SD1]) and Group B (M = [M2], SD = [SD2]). There was a significant difference in scores; t([df]) = [t-val], p = .024, d = 0.52.

ANOVA: A one-way between-subjects ANOVA was conducted to compare the effect of [Factor] on [Outcome]. There was a significant effect; F([df1], [df2]) = [val], p = .008, η² = .14. Post-hoc comparisons using the Tukey HSD test indicated significant group differences.

Correlation: [Variable 1] and [variable 2] were positively correlated, r([df]) = [val], p = .014.

Chi-square: A chi-square test of independence showed a significant association between shift timing and protocol compliance, χ²([df], N = [N]) = [val], p = .031, Cramer's V = .22.

Reporting Rule: Report exact p-values (e.g. p = .032), unless SPSS shows .000, which must be reported as p < .001. Always report effect sizes alongside significance.

Chapter 06

Write, cite and format cleanly

The Real-World Problem: Treating the literature review like an annotated shopping list ("Author A said X. Author B said Y."), or mixing personal interpretations into the Results chapter before reaching the Discussion.

Structure carries the reader. For empirical theses, follow the IMRaD framework:

SectionWhat You Must DeliverCommon Student Mistake
IntroductionProblem statement, identified research gap, objectives, significance.Starting with broad platitudes; failing to name the exact gap.
Literature ReviewThematic synthesis across themes; conceptual framework.Listing one study per paragraph with zero cross-synthesis.
MethodologyDesign, sample justification, instruments, validity/reliability, analysis plan.Too brief for an independent researcher to replicate.
ResultsObjective-by-objective presentation of tables and findings.Introducing opinions or discussing broader literature findings here.
DiscussionComparing findings with prior studies; implications and limits.Merely re-listing percentages without explaining why.
ConclusionDirect answers to research questions; recommendations.Introducing brand-new data or citations not previously presented.

The Honest Paraphrasing Test

How to Paraphrase Legally:
1. Read the source paragraph twice until you understand its underlying argument.
2. Close the browser tab or paper completely.
3. Explain the finding in your own words onto a blank screen as if teaching a peer.
4. Open the source and compare sentence structures. If your draft mimics the original word order, rewrite it from scratch.
5. Add the parenthetical in-text citation immediately. Paraphrased ideas still belong to the original author.

Pre-Submission Formatting Checklist

  • Consistent heading hierarchy (H1, H2, H3) throughout using Word Styles.
  • Every table and figure is numbered, captioned, and explicitly referenced in paragraph text ("As shown in Table 4.2...").
  • 100% two-way match: every citation in text appears in the reference list; every list item is cited.
  • Write the abstract last: 200–250 words covering Background, Methods, Core Findings (with numbers), and Conclusion.

Chapter 07

Prepare for your viva defense

The Real-World Problem: Students viewing examiners as hostile adversaries trying to "catch them out", leading to defensive arguments, ungrounded justifications, and verbal rambling.

Examiners evaluate whether you comprehend the boundaries of what you did. They test three core fundamentals:

  • Methodological Justification: Why did you select this specific approach over available alternatives?
  • Intellectual Honesty: Can you candidly identify your study's limitations before the committee points them out?
  • Core Contribution: Can you summarize what your data adds to the literature in two precise sentences?

The A-R-E Answering Framework

Answer (A) • One direct sentence: Answer directly without hedging or apologizing. ("I chose an independent samples t-test because the dependent variable was measured on a continuous scale and satisfied normality assumptions.")

Reason (R) • Academic logic: Give the cited rationale. ("According to Field (2018), when skewness and kurtosis fall between ±1.0, parametric tests provide superior statistical power.")

Example (E) • Dataset evidence: Anchor the answer in your actual thesis findings. ("In my dataset, the Shapiro-Wilk test yielded p = .18 for Group A and p = .24 for Group B, confirming acceptable normality.")

Stop talking, and let the examiner ask their next question.
Interactive Flashcard Trainer

Viva Defense Simulator

Test yourself against standard committee questions and rehearse the model A-R-E response:

Domain: Methodology Justification
"Why did you choose an independent t-test instead of a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test?"
Model A-R-E Response:
• Answer: I selected the independent samples t-test because the outcome was continuous and satisfied the parametric assumptions.
• Reason: As noted by Field (2018), parametric tests provide superior statistical power to detect true effects when distribution criteria are met.
• Example: In my sample, the Shapiro-Wilk normality test yielded p = .24 and Levene's test for equality of variance yielded p = .18, validating both assumptions.

The 7-Day Pre-Viva Countdown

  • Day 7 to 5: Re-read your Abstract, Objectives, and Conclusions until you can recite them without slides.
  • Day 4 to 3: Review every single table: know sample size, test statistic, p-value, and effect size by heart.
  • Day 2: Conduct a full timed slide rehearsal with an academic colleague or coach who interrupts with questions.
  • Day 1: Prepare your bound copy with colored adhesive tabs for instant chapter and table navigation. Rest.
